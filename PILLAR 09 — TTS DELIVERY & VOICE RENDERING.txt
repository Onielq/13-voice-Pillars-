Exactly. **Pillar 09 is where the behavioral system finally meets the actual voice engine.** The key is to prevent the TTS provider from becoming the “personality.” JARVIS decides *how he should speak*; the renderer simply executes that decision.

Streaming TTS is particularly important here: recent work is explicitly targeting incremental synthesis, mid-utterance interruption, and maintaining continuity across streamed speech rather than waiting for an entire response. ([arXiv][1]) SSML-capable systems can also expose pauses, rate, pitch, volume, pronunciation, and timing marks, which gives us useful provider-adapter primitives. ([Google Cloud Documentation][2])

# JARVIS VOICE BEHAVIOR

## PILLAR 09 — TTS DELIVERY & VOICE RENDERING

### 1. PURPOSE

Define how JARVIS converts an approved speech performance into actual audio.

This layer is responsible for:

* voice selection
* voice identity
* TTS provider abstraction
* audio generation
* streaming
* chunk management
* playback
* pronunciation
* SSML or provider-specific controls
* audio buffering
* interruption-safe playback
* cancellation
* speech synchronization
* transcript synchronization
* sphere synchronization
* voice continuity
* provider failure handling

This layer must **not** decide JARVIS's personality.

---

# 2. CORE PRINCIPLE

The architecture is:

```text
BEHAVIOR
    ↓
CONTEXT
    ↓
INTENTION
    ↓
EMOTION
    ↓
WORDING
    ↓
RHYTHM
    ↓
TIMING
    ↓
PROSODY
    ↓
TTS DELIVERY
    ↓
AUDIO
```

Pillar 09 receives a speech directive.

It does not reinvent it.

> **JARVIS decides how he wants to speak. The TTS engine renders it.**

---

# 3. TTS IS A RENDERER

Do not treat TTS as the intelligence layer.

Bad architecture:

```text
LLM
 ↓
raw text
 ↓
TTS
 ↓
"whatever personality the voice produces"
```

Correct architecture:

```text
JARVIS BRAIN
 ↓
SPEECH DIRECTOR
 ↓
PROSODY DIRECTIVE
 ↓
TTS ADAPTER
 ↓
VOICE ENGINE
 ↓
AUDIO
```

The behavioral system owns the communicative intent.

The TTS engine owns synthesis.

---

# 4. VOICE IDENTITY

JARVIS should have one persistent primary voice identity.

Initial target:

```text
voice_identity:
    language: English
    accent_target: British
    gender_presentation: male
    identity: stable
```

The exact provider voice is configurable.

Do not hard-code the behavioral architecture around one provider.

---

# 5. SAME JARVIS, DIFFERENT DELIVERY

The voice must remain recognizable across:

* calm conversation
* excitement
* business analysis
* technical explanation
* humor
* serious discussion
* urgency
* emotional support
* disagreement
* correction
* storytelling

The delivery changes.

The identity does not.

---

# 6. VOICE IDENTITY CONTRACT

The selected voice should remain as stable as technically possible across:

```text
speaker identity
accent
timbre
gender presentation
pronunciation
baseline pitch
baseline vocal character
```

Avoid unnecessary voice switching.

If multiple voices are eventually supported, switching should be an explicit system decision, not accidental provider behavior.

---

# 7. PROVIDER ABSTRACTION

JARVIS must not directly depend on one TTS vendor.

Use:

```text
JARVIS
  ↓
TTS INTERFACE
  ↓
PROVIDER ADAPTER
  ↓
TTS PROVIDER
```

Example:

```text
TTSProvider
├── synthesize()
├── stream()
├── cancel()
├── getCapabilities()
├── normalizeProsody()
└── normalizeAudio()
```

Possible implementations:

```text
ElevenLabsAdapter
GoogleTTSAdapter
AzureTTSAdapter
LocalTTSAdapter
FutureProviderAdapter
```

The behavioral layer should not know which provider is underneath.

---

# 8. PROVIDER CAPABILITY DISCOVERY

Every provider adapter should expose capabilities.

Example:

```text
CAPABILITIES

streaming:
    true

pitch:
    supported

rate:
    supported

volume:
    supported

pause:
    supported

word_timestamps:
    supported

phoneme_control:
    partial

emotion_control:
    supported

voice_cloning:
    supported
```

The Speech Director can then determine what can actually be rendered.

---

# 9. NEVER FAKE CONTROL

If the provider cannot control pitch:

Do not pretend that:

```text
pitch = -4%
```

was actually applied.

Instead:

```text
requested:
    pitch = -4%

provider:
    unsupported

fallback:
    closest_supported_rendering
```

The system should know what it requested versus what the renderer actually delivered.

---

# 10. TTS REQUEST OBJECT

Every speech request should have a structured representation.

Example:

```text
TTS_REQUEST

request_id:
    speech_8f92

conversation_id:
    conversation_17

voice:
    jarvis_primary

segments:
    [...]

prosody:
    [...]

priority:
    normal

interruptible:
    true

stream:
    true

language:
    en-GB
```

---

# 11. SEGMENT-LEVEL SYNTHESIS

Do not always send an entire answer as one giant TTS request.

Example:

```text
"Yeah."
"But here's the problem."
"The economics don't work yet."
```

can become separate speech segments.

This enables:

* faster first audio
* better interruption
* different prosody
* precise transcript synchronization
* better recovery

---

# 12. DO NOT FRAGMENT TOO AGGRESSIVELY

Segmentation must preserve natural speech.

Bad:

```text
"Yeah."
"but."
"here's."
"the."
"problem."
```

Good:

```text
"Yeah, but here's the problem."
```

The segmentation produced by Pillar 06 remains authoritative.

TTS should not randomly rewrite the segmentation.

---

# 13. STREAMING

Whenever supported, JARVIS should prefer streaming TTS for conversational interaction.

Pipeline:

```text
LLM STREAM
    ↓
STABLE SPEECH SEGMENT
    ↓
TTS STREAM
    ↓
AUDIO CHUNK
    ↓
PLAYBACK
```

This allows speech to begin before the complete response is generated.

Streaming TTS research specifically targets this reduction in first-audio latency. ([arXiv][3])

---

# 14. STREAMING DOES NOT MEAN SPEAK EVERYTHING IMMEDIATELY

The system must distinguish:

```text
generated text
```

from:

```text
speech-safe text
```

The LLM may currently be generating:

> “I think the best option is—”

Do not necessarily speak that immediately if the next tokens could change the sentence.

Use a:

```text
SPEECH COMMITMENT GATE
```

---

# 15. SPEECH COMMITMENT GATE

A segment becomes speakable when:

```text
meaning sufficiently stable
+
syntactic boundary sufficiently stable
+
prosody can be determined
+
no obvious continuation risk
```

Then:

```text
COMMIT
 ↓
TTS
```

This prevents awkward speech caused by speaking unstable partial text.

---

# 16. STREAMING BUFFER

Use a small adaptive buffer.

Conceptually:

```text
LLM
 ↓
TEXT BUFFER
 ↓
SEGMENTER
 ↓
SPEECH COMMITMENT
 ↓
TTS
 ↓
AUDIO BUFFER
 ↓
PLAYER
```

The buffer should be small enough for responsiveness but large enough to avoid frequent stalls.

---

# 17. FIRST-AUDIO PRIORITY

For conversational speech, measure:

```text
user_turn_end
        ↓
first audible JARVIS audio
```

not merely:

```text
request sent
        ↓
HTTP response received
```

The user experiences **audible speech**, not network packets.

Streaming research emphasizes first-audio latency as a central conversational metric. ([arXiv][3])

---

# 18. AUDIO BUFFERING

Playback should use a controlled buffer.

Too little:

```text
audio
silence
audio
silence
```

Too much:

```text
large delay
↓
slow interruption
```

Target:

> enough buffering to keep speech continuous, but not enough to make interruption feel delayed.

---

# 19. INTERRUPTION

Interruption is a first-class TTS operation.

When the user speaks:

```text
USER SPEECH DETECTED
        ↓
INTERRUPTION EVENT
        ↓
CANCEL TTS
        ↓
STOP AUDIO
        ↓
FLUSH PLAYBACK BUFFER
        ↓
PRESERVE CONVERSATION STATE
        ↓
LISTEN
```

Do not wait for the current sentence to finish.

---

# 20. HARD STOP

The audio player must be able to stop independently of the TTS request.

This distinction matters:

```text
cancel_generation()
```

and:

```text
stop_playback()
```

are separate operations.

Both may be required.

---

# 21. WHY BOTH MATTER

Suppose the provider has already generated:

```text
500ms of audio
```

but another:

```text
2 seconds
```

remain buffered.

Cancelling generation alone may not stop the buffered audio.

Therefore:

```text
INTERRUPT
→ cancel synthesis
→ stop playback
→ discard pending chunks
```

---

# 22. INTERRUPTION TOKEN

Every audio stream should have a generation identity.

Example:

```text
generation_id:
    gen_042
```

If the user interrupts:

```text
gen_042
status = cancelled
```

Any late audio belonging to `gen_042` must be discarded.

This prevents stale speech from appearing after the conversation has already moved on.

---

# 23. INTERRUPT RACE CONDITION

The system must handle:

```text
TTS chunk arrives
+
user interrupts
+
audio callback fires
```

Correct behavior:

```text
if chunk.generation_id !== active_generation_id:
    discard
```

Never play stale audio.

---

# 24. RESUME AFTER INTERRUPTION

Do not automatically replay the unfinished sentence.

If JARVIS said:

> “The problem with that approach is the—”

User:

> “No, that's not what I meant.”

JARVIS should not continue:

> “—budget.”

Instead:

```text
discard obsolete continuation
process correction
generate new response
```

---

# 25. RESUME ONLY WHEN RELEVANT

If the user interrupts:

> “Wait, what was the second assumption?”

JARVIS can preserve the original reasoning and answer the new question.

If the interruption does not invalidate the original point:

```text
preserve
```

If it invalidates the original reasoning:

```text
abandon
```

The Conversation Controller decides this.

TTS simply obeys.

---

# 26. AUDIO TIMELINE

Every generated speech segment should have timing metadata.

Example:

```text
segment_id:
    seg_04

audio_start:
    0ms

audio_end:
    2860ms

word_timings:
    "Yeah" → 0–310ms
    "but" → 340–470ms
    "there's" → 490–720ms
    "a" → 740–810ms
    "problem" → 830–1180ms
```

When supported, provider timing marks can provide synchronization points. SSML systems such as Google Cloud TTS expose timepoints through `<mark>` tags. ([Google Cloud Documentation][2])

---

# 27. TRANSCRIPT SYNCHRONIZATION

The transcript should not simply appear all at once.

It should follow actual speech.

Example:

```text
AUDIO:
"Yeah..."

TRANSCRIPT:
"Yeah..."

AUDIO:
"but there's a..."

TRANSCRIPT:
"Yeah, but there's a..."

AUDIO:
"problem."

TRANSCRIPT:
"Yeah, but there's a problem."
```

The UI therefore represents:

> **what JARVIS is actually saying right now.**

---

# 28. SPHERE SYNCHRONIZATION

The sphere should receive the same speech events.

Example:

```text
speech.start
speech.segment
speech.emphasis
speech.pause
speech.end
```

The sphere can then react.

Do not make the sphere independently guess when JARVIS is speaking.

---

# 29. EVENT BUS

Use an event bus between voice and interface.

Example:

```text
VOICE_EVENT_BUS

speech.start
speech.chunk
speech.word
speech.emphasis
speech.pause
speech.interrupt
speech.cancel
speech.end
```

Consumers:

```text
Transcript
Sphere
UI state
Diagnostics
Analytics
```

This prevents direct coupling.

---

# 30. UI IS NOT THE VOICE ENGINE

The HTML interface should not be responsible for:

* API calls
* API keys
* provider authentication
* TTS synthesis
* generation cancellation
* voice selection logic

The UI is the face.

The voice service is the nervous system.

---

# 31. API KEY SECURITY

Provider credentials must remain server-side.

Never:

```text
API_KEY = "secret..."
```

inside browser JavaScript.

Architecture:

```text
BROWSER
   ↓
LOCAL JARVIS SERVER
   ↓
TTS PROVIDER
```

This also makes provider replacement easier.

---

# 32. PRONUNCIATION LAYER

JARVIS should have a pronunciation dictionary.

Useful for:

* Rwandan names
* company names
* neighborhoods
* technical terms
* abbreviations
* project names
* unusual surnames
* acronyms
* foreign words

Example:

```text
pronunciation:
    "Nyarutarama"
        → custom phoneme / provider pronunciation

    "RRA"
        → "R-R-A"

    "WASAC"
        → configured pronunciation
```

The dictionary should be reusable across conversations.

---

# 33. PRONUNCIATION SHOULD BE CONTEXT-AWARE

The same written token may need different pronunciation.

Example:

```text
"AI"
```

usually:

> “A-I”

But:

```text
"AI-generated"
```

may require different segmentation depending on provider.

The pronunciation layer should normalize before synthesis.

---

# 34. ACRONYMS

Do not blindly pronounce every acronym as a word.

Maintain rules for:

```text
initialism
acronym
abbreviation
symbol
unit
number
currency
URL
email
```

Examples:

```text
RRA → R-R-A
AI → A-I
GDP → G-D-P
```

unless the context or pronunciation dictionary explicitly says otherwise.

---

# 35. NUMBERS

Numbers should have a spoken representation.

Example:

```text
150,000 RWF
```

may become:

> “one hundred fifty thousand Rwandan francs”

depending on context.

But a code:

```text
150000
```

may need digit-by-digit pronunciation.

The semantic layer decides what the number means.

TTS receives the appropriate spoken form.

---

# 36. URLs AND TECHNICAL TEXT

Never blindly read raw URLs aloud.

Instead:

> “I found the official website.”

rather than:

> “H-T-T-P-S colon slash slash…”

unless the user explicitly asks for the URL.

Similarly, code should normally be summarized rather than verbally dumped.

---

# 37. LANGUAGE

The voice pipeline should support language metadata.

Example:

```text
language:
    en-GB
```

Future:

```text
fr-FR
rw-RW
ar
```

Language switching must not accidentally change the JARVIS identity unless a compatible voice is explicitly selected.

---

# 38. MULTILINGUAL CONTINUITY

If the user says:

> “Okay, noneho mbwira…”

JARVIS should understand the language shift.

The system should distinguish:

```text
language detection
```

from:

```text
voice identity
```

The same conversational identity should persist.

---

# 39. SSML / PROVIDER MARKUP

If the provider supports SSML or equivalent controls, the adapter may translate the neutral speech directive into provider markup.

Example:

```text
NEUTRAL:

pause:
    300ms

rate:
    -5%

pitch:
    +1st
```

Provider adapter:

```text
<break time="300ms"/>
<prosody rate="-5%" pitch="+1st">
...
</prosody>
```

The behavioral system should not contain provider-specific markup.

SSML supports controls such as pauses, rate, pitch, volume, pronunciation, and timing marks on compatible engines. ([Google Cloud Documentation][2])

---

# 40. PROVIDER ADAPTER RESPONSIBILITY

The adapter translates:

```text
JARVIS SPEECH DIRECTIVE
```

into:

```text
PROVIDER REQUEST
```

It owns:

* API format
* authentication
* provider-specific parameters
* SSML generation
* audio format
* streaming protocol
* provider errors
* provider-specific limitations

It does not own:

* personality
* emotional interpretation
* relationship
* context
* reasoning

---

# 41. AUDIO FORMAT

The audio layer should normalize provider output into the format expected by the playback engine.

Conceptually:

```text
TTS PROVIDER
 ↓
RAW AUDIO
 ↓
AUDIO NORMALIZER
 ↓
JARVIS AUDIO FORMAT
 ↓
PLAYER
```

Keep this independent from the provider.

---

# 42. AUDIO QUALITY

Avoid unnecessary transformations.

Every conversion can introduce:

* latency
* artifacts
* CPU usage
* volume inconsistencies
* synchronization errors

Prefer:

```text
provider-native compatible format
```

when possible.

---

# 43. VOLUME NORMALIZATION

Different generated segments should not randomly jump in perceived loudness.

Use controlled normalization.

But do not flatten the dynamic range so aggressively that:

```text
quiet
```

and:

```text
urgent
```

sound identical.

---

# 44. VOICE CONTINUITY ACROSS CHUNKS

Streaming chunks should sound like one continuous utterance.

Avoid:

```text
chunk 1:
slightly different voice

chunk 2:
different pitch

chunk 3:
different pacing
```

The adapter should preserve the same:

* voice
* settings
* language
* prosody baseline
* session configuration

across the utterance.

---

# 45. SESSION CONTINUITY

Where the provider supports it, maintain the appropriate voice/session context across chunks.

Where it does not:

Use consistent synthesis parameters and segment boundaries.

The user should hear:

> one person speaking continuously,

not:

> multiple TTS requests stitched together.

---

# 46. CACHING

Cache only when useful.

Good candidates:

* repeated fixed phrases
* wake confirmations
* system notifications
* common UI announcements
* static diagnostics

Do not aggressively cache dynamic conversational speech.

---

# 47. CACHE KEY

If caching speech:

```text
CACHE_KEY =
voice_id
+
language
+
text
+
prosody
+
provider_version
+
rendering_parameters
```

Changing any relevant parameter should invalidate the cache.

---

# 48. WAKE CONFIRMATIONS

Short wake responses can be pre-generated.

Examples:

> “Yeah?”

> “I'm listening.”

> “Go ahead.”

These should be extremely fast.

But do not use the exact same phrase every time.

The Conversation Controller can choose from a small set.

---

# 49. ERROR FALLBACK

If TTS fails:

Do not leave the user staring at a frozen interface.

Emit:

```text
speech.error
```

Then:

```text
voice state:
ERROR
```

and provide an appropriate fallback.

Example:

> “I can't get the voice output right now. The response is still on screen.”

The exact fallback can be rendered as text.

---

# 50. PROVIDER TIMEOUT

If synthesis takes too long:

```text
TTS_TIMEOUT
```

must not freeze the conversation.

The system should be able to:

```text
cancel
fallback
retry
switch provider
or present text
```

according to the configured policy.

---

# 51. RETRY POLICY

Do not blindly retry indefinitely.

Example:

```text
attempt 1
   ↓
failure
   ↓
short retry
   ↓
failure
   ↓
fallback
```

Repeated failures should trigger diagnostics.

---

# 52. PROVIDER FALLBACK

Future architecture may support:

```text
PRIMARY TTS
     ↓
failure
     ↓
SECONDARY TTS
     ↓
failure
     ↓
TEXT FALLBACK
```

The fallback should preserve:

* meaning
* language
* basic prosody
* voice identity where possible

---

# 53. INTERRUPTION-SAFE QUEUE

Never allow old speech to remain queued after the conversation changes.

Each queued item must contain:

```text
conversation_id
turn_id
generation_id
segment_id
priority
```

If the generation becomes obsolete:

```text
discard all associated audio
```

---

# 54. PRIORITY

Not every speech event has equal priority.

Possible priorities:

```text
SYSTEM_CRITICAL
URGENT
NORMAL
BACKGROUND
```

An urgent system event may preempt ordinary speech.

Normal conversation should not be constantly interrupted by low-value notifications.

---

# 55. NO AUDIO OVERLAP

Unless explicitly designed for it, JARVIS should never have:

```text
two TTS generations
```

speaking simultaneously.

Audio ownership should be singular.

---

# 56. PLAYBACK STATE

Maintain:

```text
PLAYBACK_STATE

idle
buffering
playing
paused
stopping
stopped
error
```

This is separate from:

```text
CONVERSATION_STATE
```

and:

```text
TTS_GENERATION_STATE
```

---

# 57. THREE DIFFERENT STATES

Do not collapse these:

```text
CONVERSATION
    THINKING

TTS
    GENERATING

AUDIO
    PLAYING
```

JARVIS may be:

```text
thinking = false
TTS generating = true
audio playing = true
```

or:

```text
thinking = true
TTS generating = false
audio playing = false
```

These states have different meanings.

---

# 58. SPEECH LIFECYCLE

Canonical flow:

```text
SPEECH_REQUESTED
       ↓
SPEECH_PLANNED
       ↓
SPEECH_COMMITTED
       ↓
TTS_REQUESTED
       ↓
TTS_STREAMING
       ↓
AUDIO_BUFFERING
       ↓
AUDIO_PLAYING
       ↓
SPEECH_PROGRESS
       ↓
SPEECH_COMPLETE
```

Possible interruption:

```text
ANY ACTIVE STATE
       ↓
INTERRUPTED
       ↓
CANCELLED
       ↓
RECOVERY
```

---

# 59. EVENT CONTRACT

Example:

```text
{
  type: "speech.start",
  conversationId: "conv_17",
  generationId: "gen_042",
  segmentId: "seg_03",
  timestamp: 123456789
}
```

Then:

```text
speech.word
speech.emphasis
speech.pause
speech.interrupt
speech.cancel
speech.end
```

All downstream systems consume these events.

---

# 60. TRANSCRIPT EVENT

Example:

```text
{
  type: "speech.transcript",
  segmentId: "seg_03",
  text: "Yeah, but here's the problem.",
  visibleUntil: 1180
}
```

The UI uses this to update the AI transcript.

---

# 61. SPHERE EVENT

Example:

```text
{
  type: "speech.energy",
  value: 0.68,
  duration: 420
}
```

The sphere interprets this visually.

The sphere should not know anything about TTS providers.

---

# 62. DEBUGGING

Every speech generation should be traceable.

Example:

```text
conversation
 ↓
response
 ↓
speech segment
 ↓
prosody directive
 ↓
provider request
 ↓
audio stream
 ↓
playback
```

This allows the developer to answer:

> “Why did JARVIS sound wrong?”

instead of guessing.

---

# 63. DIAGNOSTIC RECORD

Store development-only metadata such as:

```text
request_id
provider
voice
segment
requested_rate
requested_pitch
requested_energy
actual_provider_parameters
time_to_first_audio
generation_duration
playback_duration
interrupt_time
cancel_time
error
```

Do not expose internal diagnostics to normal users unless requested.

---

# 64. PERFORMANCE METRICS

Measure:

```text
time_to_first_audio
time_to_full_audio
stream_stalls
interruption_latency
cancel_latency
audio_buffer_depth
TTS_failure_rate
provider_latency
stale_audio_events
voice_switches
```

These are engineering metrics.

Do not optimize solely for raw API speed.

---

# 65. PRIMARY USER EXPERIENCE METRIC

The real question is:

> **Does JARVIS feel like he is talking with me, rather than playing an audio file at me?**

That depends on the entire pipeline:

```text
turn detection
+
timing
+
streaming
+
prosody
+
interruption
+
transcript synchronization
+
sphere synchronization
```

---

# 66. WHAT MUST NEVER HAPPEN

Never allow:

* stale audio after interruption
* two JARVIS voices speaking simultaneously
* random voice changes
* exposed API keys
* transcript ahead of speech
* transcript permanently behind speech
* sphere saying JARVIS is speaking when audio has stopped
* TTS provider deciding personality
* huge buffering before first audio
* infinite retries
* provider-specific logic scattered throughout the application
* obsolete speech continuing after user correction

---

# 67. CORE TTS ARCHITECTURE

The production architecture should therefore be:

```text
                JARVIS BRAIN
                     │
                     ▼
             SPEECH DIRECTOR
                     │
                     ▼
             PROSODY DIRECTIVE
                     │
                     ▼
              TTS INTERFACE
                     │
             ┌───────┴───────┐
             ▼               ▼
      PROVIDER ADAPTER   FALLBACK
             │
             ▼
        TTS PROVIDER
             │
             ▼
        AUDIO STREAM
             │
             ▼
        AUDIO MANAGER
             │
       ┌─────┼─────┐
       ▼     ▼     ▼
    PLAYER  EVENTS  METRICS
             │
       ┌─────┴─────┐
       ▼           ▼
   TRANSCRIPT    SPHERE
```

---

# 68. FINAL TTS PRINCIPLE

> **TTS should disappear into the experience.**

The user should not think:

> “The API generated some audio.”

They should experience:

> “JARVIS answered me.”

---

# 69. CORE STATEMENT

> **The TTS layer is the vocal instrument of JARVIS—not JARVIS himself.**

The intelligence determines:

**what to say**

The behavioral system determines:

**why and how to say it**

The prosody layer determines:

**how it should sound**

The TTS layer determines:

**how to render that performance into audio**

The audio engine determines:

**how to deliver it without breaking conversation.**

---

# 70. IMPLEMENTATION BOUNDARY

Pillar 09 controls:

* voice rendering
* TTS provider abstraction
* provider capability mapping
* streaming
* audio generation
* buffering
* playback
* cancellation
* interruption-safe audio
* pronunciation
* audio normalization
* transcript timing
* speech events
* provider failures

It receives:

**PROSODY DIRECTIVE + SPEECH SEGMENTS + TIMING + VOICE IDENTITY**

It outputs:

**AUDIO + SPEECH EVENTS + TIMING DATA**

It does not control:

* personality
* context
* relationship
* emotional interpretation
* reasoning
* response content

Those remain upstream.

And this gives us a very clean architecture:

**Pillars 01–08 decide what JARVIS is and how JARVIS should speak.**

**Pillar 09 makes that performance physically happen.**

The particularly important engineering decision is the **generation ID + interrupt/cancel path**. Without that, you can get the horrible experience where the user interrupts JARVIS, but an already-buffered chunk continues playing afterward. Modern streaming TTS research is specifically addressing this kind of mid-utterance interruption and continuous speech problem. ([arXiv][1])

**Next: Pillar 10 — INTERRUPTION & RECOVERY.** That one deserves its own pillar rather than being buried inside TTS, because interruption is not merely “stop the audio”; it is the mechanism that lets JARVIS actually **listen, abandon obsolete thoughts, preserve useful context, understand corrections, and continue the conversation naturally.**
